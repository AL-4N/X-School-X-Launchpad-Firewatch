Main Concern
FFMC, DMC and DC track fuel moisture at increasing depths and timescales; ISI and BUI describe spread rate and fuel availability; FWI combines them into overall fire intensity potential. The composite score above blends this weather-danger signal with real satellite fire-detection proximity, intensity trend, and wind amplification into one auditable 0–100 number. This estimate uses standard start-up values rather than multi-day carried-forward moisture history, so deep-drought effects (DC) may be understated.

Satellite passes detect thermal anomalies (active fire pixels), not smoke, imagery texture, or burn scars. Detections within ~2km are grouped into clusters so repeated passes over one fire don't read as several. FRP (Fire Radiative Power) approximates energy release; rising total FRP across consecutive passes over the same area indicates an intensifying, not just persistent, fire.

WILDFIRE SURVIVAL GUIDE

Before a Fire

What to do now, before fire season puts you under pressure.

  • Create defensible space. Clear dry brush, leaves, and debris at least 30 ft (9 m) from your home. Zone 1 (0–30 ft) should be lean, clean, and green.
  • Harden your home. Cover roof vents with fine wire mesh. Use non-combustible materials on decks and eaves — embers land there first.
  • Know your evacuation zone. Look up your zone at ready.gov or your county's OES site. Know two routes out — roads block fast.
  • Build a go-bag. 3-day supply of water, medications, important documents, cash, phone charger, N95 masks, and a change of clothes. Keep it by the door.
  • Register for alerts. Sign up for your county's emergency notification system. Many evacuations happen overnight — you need a wake-up call, not a news alert.
  • Back up documents digitally. Scan IDs, insurance, medical records, and store them in cloud storage you can access from any phone.
  • Leave early — not when you see flames. Roads clog in minutes. Smoke can make driving impossible within hours. Go when ordered, not when scared.
  • Take the bag, leave everything else. Once you're out, don't go back. Houses can be rebuilt. Don't risk getting trapped.
  • Text, don't call. Texts route around congested networks. Calling ties up lines emergency services need.
  • Drive with windows up, headlights on, AC on recirculate. This keeps smoke out and makes you visible in low visibility conditions.
  • If you're trapped in a building, close all vents and doors, seal gaps with wet towels, turn off HVAC, and signal rescuers from a window or roof.
  • Never shelter in your car in the open. If you must pull over, stay low below window level and cover yourself with a wool blanket. Call 911 and report your location.
  • Wait for official clearance. Hot spots remain active for days. Carbon monoxide, unstable structures, and downed power lines are invisible killers.
  • Document everything before cleaning. Photograph all damage thoroughly for insurance before moving or discarding anything.
  • Wear N95 when cleaning ash. Ash from burned homes contains heavy metals, asbestos, and carcinogens — not just soot. Double-bag debris before disposal.
  • Don't drink tap water without checking. Wildfire heat can warp plastic pipes and contaminate water mains miles from the burn area. Wait for official confirmation.
  • Inspect before entering. Look for cracks in the foundation, smell for gas, check that the roof isn't compromised. Have a pro assess structural integrity first.
  • Wildfire trauma is real. PTSD, anxiety, and grief are common after losing a home or community. Local mental health resources and SAMHSA (1-800-662-4357) can help.
